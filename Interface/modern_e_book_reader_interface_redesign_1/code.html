INTRODUCTION TO THIS EDITION

I DON'T KNOW what it is like to read this book. I only know what it was like to live the writing of it.

I moved to the United States in 1992. Something was born then, in a corner of my mind. There were some isolated ideas that I knew were important, but that didn't seem to have any relation to each other: two men meeting on a plane; a car on the ice; the relevance of coin tricks and, above all, the United States — that strange and immense place where I was now living, and which I knew I didn't understand. However, I wanted to understand it. More than that: I wanted to describe it.

It was during a brief stay in Iceland, where I watched a model about the voyages of Leif Erickson, that everything clicked. I wrote a letter to my agent and my editor explaining what the story of the book would be. I noted "American Gods" at the top of the letter, confident that I would come up with a better title.

A few weeks later, my editor sent me a sketch of the cover. It was a road with a lightning bolt striking right in the middle, and at the top it said "American Gods". It looked like the cover of the book I had thought about writing.

12

It was at the same time disconcerting and electrifying to see the cover before the book. I hung it on the wall and looked at it, intimidated, and any possibility of thinking of another title disappeared forever. That was the book cover. That was the book. I just needed to write it.

I finished the first chapter during a train trip from Chicago to San Diego. And I continued traveling, and continued writing. I drove from Minneapolis to Florida on back roads, traversing paths that I imagined Shadow would take in the book. I wrote and, at times, when I got stuck, I hit the road. I ate pasties in the Upper Peninsula of Michigan and hushpuppies in Cairo, Illinois. I tried my best not to write about any place I hadn't been through.

I wrote my book in many places — houses in Florida, a cabin in Wisconsin, a hotel room in Las Vegas.

My narrative followed Shadow's journey, and, when I didn't know where he had gotten himself into, I wrote a story about the "Coming to America", and, when I finished, I had discovered Shadow's whereabouts, then went back to him. I wanted to write two thousand words a day, but gave myself satisfied with a thousand.

I remember that, upon finishing the first draft of the book, I spoke with Gene Wolfe — the wisest writer I know and with more excellent novels than anyone else I've ever conversed with — that I thought I had finally learned how to write novels. He faced me and gave a gentle smile. "No one learns how to write novels," he explained. "We only learn to write the novel we are working on."
